02

The rule behind every finding

Justin's ask: every finding in the report has to show its work. Not just a headline number, but where it came from and what's still unknown. Three parts: the raw data, what we make of it, and the question it leaves open. A few of the findings built this way so far:

Processing capacity often sits idle

Raw data

Processing capacity is usually rented, not owned, and often sits unused.

Analysis

This came from Claude's read of the interviews, and it matched Deme's own analysis independently.

Open question

Some of this could turn into new products, mucilage came up, but we don't know if there's a market for it yet.

Owning your own manufacturing is the exception, not the rule

Raw data

Choba Choba doesn't make its own chocolate, Conexión tolls out all of its manufacturing, and Rights Chocolate, which does own a factory, uses only 10 to 20 percent of it.

Analysis

Owning processing capacity is rare across this pipeline, and where it exists it's often underused rather than fully needed.

Open question

Could Rights Chocolate's spare capacity actually serve smaller players like Kamm? We haven't mapped total capacity across the pipeline to check yet.

Amazon sourcing is real, but only partial

Raw data

Only about six of the companies we interviewed currently source directly from Amazon basin regions. Several strong candidates source from elsewhere in the same four countries.

Analysis

Amazon sourcing is a real thread through the pipeline, but it's the exception among the companies we've talked to so far, not the norm.

Open question

How strict should the Amazon-sourcing bar be? Ancestral Organics stayed in the pipeline because its Amazon pilot is about a year out. That was a judgment call, not a written rule yet.

Vertical integration means control, not ownership

Raw data

GoodSAM owns no farms and no factories anywhere in its supply chain.

Analysis

Under an ownership test, GoodSAM looks like a weak fit. Under a control test, setting sourcing terms, quality standards, and owning the buyer relationship, it's one of the most integrated companies in the pipeline. Rick pushed us to recode it this way.

Open question

Has the master sheet actually been recoded under this new definition for every company yet? Not confirmed.

Rick tested the structure live against the processing example and it held. His words: "I like the structure. That's validated." One change made on the call: the middle column used to be called "reading." Rick pointed out Claude is actually interpreting the data, not just summarizing it, so we're calling it "analysis" now.

04

Grouping the companies: still not settled

The point of the archetypes is to understand how the market actually breaks down, spot where companies could work together, and help decide where the fund should invest. Rick's framework, from the July 23 call: upstream, downstream, companies that support the chain without buying or selling cacao themselves, a real cluster that's vertically integrated, and an open bucket for anything that doesn't fit cleanly.

Here's a first pass at sorting the actual pipeline into it. This is a draft to react to, not a final answer.

Vertically integrated: buys directly from farmers, sells a finished or branded product

  • Choba Choba, Rick's own example, its cacao goes into Felchlin's Grand Cru line as a semi-finished ingredient
  • GoodSAM Foods, owns no farms or factories, but controls sourcing, quality, and the brand end to end
  • Rights Chocolate, owns its own factory, sells its own branded bars
  • Ancestral Organics, buys from 254 smallholders, sells 30 plus branded SKUs
  • Sumar Limitada, Chocolate Baure, buys wild harvest cacao, sells only finished product under its own brand
  • Kamm, buys from 48 Chachi families, makes and sells its own chocolate and byproduct line
  • Cacao Bisiesto, owns its own farm and trades on top of it
  • República del Cacao, same pattern, though it can't take direct investment since it's part of Groupe Savencia

Chain support: helps the chain work without buying or selling cacao itself

  • Hola Tractor, Rick's own example, a tractor marketplace that never touches the crop
  • Ecuanibs, draft call, worth checking with Rick directly: it owns no land or processing and earns a flat commission connecting associations to chocolate makers, closer to a broker than a buyer or seller

Downstream, still fuzzy, worth pinning down together

  • Conexión Chocolate, buys from cooperatives and tolls out its manufacturing, but sells under its own brand. This is the company Rick flagged as mislabeled, we haven't settled where it actually sits
  • Franchy, processes cacao and most likely sells to other chocolate makers rather than running its own consumer brand, needs confirming

Reference example, not live pipeline

  • Uncommon Cacao, the company Rick used on the call to describe pure midstream trading. Already a case study rather than a live deal, since it's merging into Silva

Nothing here is locked yet. Nuna Labs and ARCASY still aren't represented, we're missing meeting notes for both. Conexión and Franchy need a real answer, not a guess. We need a dedicated session to define these categories properly and check every company against them, before the July 27 call with Rick.

06

New this week: a profile for every company

Idea from the call: one document per company covering its archetype, key insights, location, and capital needs, so nobody has to go dig through the spreadsheet. Rick expanded it into three formats: a three to five page profile, a longer ten page case study for lessons learned, and a shorter one-pager.

His point about it, worth keeping in mind: the value isn't that we can generate these fast now. It's that actually writing one forces you to notice what you don't know yet and go back and ask.
